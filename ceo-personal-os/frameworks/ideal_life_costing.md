# Ideal Lifestyle Costing

*Adapted from Tim Ferriss's lifestyle design concepts in The 4-Hour Workweek. The core insight: most people set income goals without knowing what those numbers are actually for. When you calculate the real cost of the life you want — not a fantasy life, but your actual ideal normal life — the number is often different from what you assumed. Sometimes lower. Sometimes higher. Either way, knowing it changes how you make career and financial decisions.*

---

## What This Is

Most people work backward from a salary number: "I need to make $X." But they never define what $X is supposed to buy. This framework flips it: first you design the life, then you calculate the cost, then you figure out how to fund it.

This is especially useful during a career transition, when the temptation is to take the first offer that matches your old salary, regardless of whether that salary actually matches the life you want.

## Why This Matters for You

You're looking for a new job. That means you'll be evaluating offers. The usual approach is to compare comp packages. The better approach is to know — with real numbers — what your ideal life actually costs, and then evaluate offers against that number.

This isn't about luxury. It's about clarity. The question is: "What does my version of a good life cost per month?"

## When to Use This

- During a career transition (now)
- During your annual review
- Any time you're making a major financial decision (move, job change, investment)

---

## Part 1: Define Your Ideal Normal

This is not your dream vacation or your fantasy retirement. This is your ideal **normal Tuesday**. A day you'd be happy to repeat for years.

Describe it briefly:

**Where do you live?** (City, type of home, owned/rented)


**What does your morning look like?**


**What kind of work are you doing?** (In-office, remote, hybrid? Hours per day?)


**What do you do for health/fitness?** (Gym membership, personal trainer, sports, gear?)


**What does family time look like?** (Activities, meals, travel frequency)


**What do you do for fun?** (Hobbies, entertainment, social activities)


**What does your spouse's life look like?** (Working, not working, pursuing something specific?)


**What do your kids need?** (School, activities, support)

## Part 4: Reflection

**Is this number higher or lower than you expected?**


**How does this compare to what you earned last year?**


**What could you adjust to lower the number without sacrificing what matters?**


**What are you currently spending money on that doesn't appear in your ideal life?**


**What appears in your ideal life that you're not currently spending on?**


**Knowing this number, what does it tell you about the kind of job you should be looking for?**


---

## Part 5: Decision Filter

Now you have a number. Use it:

- When evaluating job offers: "Does this total comp cover my Ideal Life number with room to spare?"
- When negotiating: "I know exactly what I need. I'm not guessing."
- When considering trade-offs: "Would I take $20K less for full remote? Let me check against my number."
- When feeling pressure: "I don't need to panic-accept. I know my floor."

---

## Key Insight

The point of this exercise is not to be cheap or to be lavish. The point is to remove vagueness from financial decisions. Most career anxiety comes from not knowing what you actually need. Once you know, the anxiety doesn't disappear — but it gets specific. And specific problems are solvable.

---

*Update this during your annual review or any time your life circumstances change significantly. Your number will evolve as your kids grow, your housing changes, or your priorities shift.*

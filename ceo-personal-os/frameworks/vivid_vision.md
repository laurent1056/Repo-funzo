# Vivid Vision

*Inspired by Tony Robbins' visualization techniques and Cameron Herold's Vivid Vision methodology. The core idea: if you can describe your ideal future in specific, sensory detail — as if you're already living it — your brain begins to organize behavior around making it real. Vague goals produce vague results. Vivid descriptions produce directed action.*

---

## What This Is

A Vivid Vision is a detailed description of your life at a specific point in the future, written in present tense, as if you're already there. It is not a goal list. It is a story about a day in your life, three years from now, where everything has gone well.

This is not delusional optimism. It is directed imagination. You're not predicting the future — you're deciding what to aim at with enough specificity that you'll recognize it when you see it.

## Why It Works

Your brain doesn't distinguish well between a vividly imagined experience and a real one. When you write a detailed picture of your future, you create a reference point. Decisions become easier: "Does this move me toward that picture or away from it?"

CEOs do this for companies all the time — it's called a strategic vision. This is the same exercise, applied to your life.

## When to Use This

- During your annual review
- After a major life transition
- Whenever you feel like you're drifting without direction
- Update it once a year. Read it quarterly.

---

## Instructions

Set aside 60-90 minutes. Find a quiet place. No phone, no interruptions.

Write in **present tense** ("I am..." not "I will...").
Write in **specific detail** (not "I'm healthy" but "I wake up at 6:15 without an alarm, my back doesn't hurt, I can keep up with my kids at the park without getting winded").
Write about **feelings**, not just facts ("I feel calm when I wake up" not just "I wake up early").

Describe a single, specific day in your life — three years from now — where things have gone well.

---

## My Vivid Vision: A Day in My Life — February 2029

*Replace everything below with your own version. This is an example based on your current priorities.*

### Morning

I wake up at 6:00 AM in a home that feels settled — not temporary, not transitional. My body feels good. I slept well because I've been consistent about shutting down screens by 9 PM and getting to bed by 10. I'm at a weight I'm comfortable with, not because I'm obsessed with a number, but because I've been moving my body regularly for three years now and it shows.

I make coffee and sit with it for fifteen minutes before anyone else is up. This is my time. I'm not scrolling. I'm not anxious about the day. I know what I'm doing today because I planned it yesterday.

### Work

I'm in a role that uses my experience as a CEO and founder, but the pressure is different now — it's challenge, not crisis. I'm doing work that matters to me. I chose this job deliberately, not out of desperation. My colleagues respect me, and I respect them. I'm compensated well — not just enough to get by, but enough that financial stress is no longer a constant background hum.

I have clear boundaries between work and home. When I close the laptop, I'm done. I've learned that my value isn't measured by my availability.

### Family

When I get home, I'm present. My wife and I have dinner together most nights. We talk — not just logistics, but real conversation. She would say I'm a better listener than I was three years ago. I would agree.

My kids know they can count on me. Not because I'm perfect, but because I show up. I'm at their events. I ask about their lives and actually listen to the answers. They see a dad who takes care of himself, works hard, and is honest about his struggles. That's the model I want to give them.

### Evening

After the kids are settled, my wife and I have time together that isn't just the leftover scraps of the day. We've built rituals — a walk, a show we watch together, a conversation on the porch. The marriage feels like a partnership again, not a co-management arrangement.

I go to bed feeling like I used the day well. Not perfectly — but well.

### The Feeling

The dominant feeling in this future is **settled clarity**. I'm not chasing. I'm not performing. I'm not anxious about what's next because I'm genuinely engaged with what's now. The restlessness that defined my transition period has been replaced by purposeful calm.

## Reflection Questions

After writing your Vivid Vision, answer these:

**What is the single biggest difference between that future and today?**


**What would need to be true for that day to be a normal Tuesday, not a fantasy?**


**What is the first concrete step toward that picture?**


**What is the biggest obstacle between here and there?**


**Who do you need to become to live that day?**


---

## How to Use This Going Forward

1. Save this file after writing your version.
2. Re-read it at the start of each quarter.
3. During quarterly reviews, ask: "Is my current trajectory pointed at this picture?"
4. Update it once a year during your annual review. Your vision will evolve — that's a sign of growth, not inconsistency.

---

*The goal is not to predict the future. The goal is to decide what's worth building — and then notice whether your daily choices are building it.*
